Managed Service 1
SLA Framework: Contractual Accountability for Production Reliability
T-Mat Global's managed DevOps SLA framework defines four categories of commitment that govern the engagement: availability SLAs (infrastructure uptime targets with measurement methodology and credit terms for SLA breaches); incident response SLAs (acknowledgment, triage, and resolution time targets by incident severity — P1 acknowledgment within 15 minutes, P2 within 60 minutes, P3 within 4 hours); change management SLAs (lead time commitments for infrastructure changes, change failure rate targets, and post-change validation requirements); and delivery SLAs (pipeline availability commitments, deployment frequency targets, and DORA metric improvement commitments over the engagement term). The SLA framework is not a best-effort service level description — it is a contractual commitment that creates economic accountability for T-Mat Global to meet the reliability standard the client's engineering organization requires. The SLA framework is reviewed quarterly with the client CTO and updated to reflect the evolution of the infrastructure and the engineering organization's changing reliability requirements.
Managed Service 2
24/7 Monitoring: Event-Driven Alerting That Finds Issues Before Users Do
T-Mat Global's 24/7 monitoring framework for managed DevOps clients implements the observability coverage required to detect infrastructure issues before they become user-facing incidents — derived from the monitoring architecture that T-Mobile USA applied to production systems serving hundreds of millions of users. The monitoring stack covers: application-layer monitoring with SLO-based error budget burn rate alerting (detecting degradation trends before the SLO budget is exhausted); infrastructure-layer monitoring with the four golden signals collected at every service boundary; synthetic monitoring from outside the infrastructure boundary (detecting availability failures that internal health checks miss); and capacity monitoring with predictive threshold alerting (detecting resource saturation trends before the saturation produces latency degradation). Alert routing follows a tiered model: automated remediation for the issue categories with known-good automated responses; T-Mat Global on-call engineer for issues requiring human judgment but not requiring client engineering team involvement; and client engineering team escalation only for issues that require application-layer intervention beyond T-Mat Global's infrastructure-layer accountability. Read the full SRE framework at T-Mat Global's SRE guide.
Managed Service 3
Incident Response: MTTR Measured in Minutes, Not Hours
T-Mat Global's incident response framework for managed DevOps clients targets MTTR measured in minutes for the P1 and P2 incident categories that determine whether the client's engineering organization meets its availability commitments to the business. The framework covers four phases: detection (automated alerting within 60 seconds of a threshold breach, with correlated alerts that identify the root cause category before the on-call engineer is paged); triage (on-call engineer assessment against the pre-written runbook for the alert category, with automated diagnostic collection reducing the time from alert to root cause hypothesis); remediation (automated remediation for the runbook-covered categories, manual remediation with step-by-step runbook guidance for the categories requiring human intervention); and post-incident review (structured blameless post-mortem with timeline reconstruction, root cause analysis, and improvement action tracking within 48 hours of every P1 and P2 incident). The MTTR target T-Mat Global holds itself to is not based on industry benchmarks — it is based on the T-Mobile USA production incident response standard that Sainath Mitalakar operated before founding T-Mat Global.
Managed Service 4
Capacity Management: Infrastructure That Scales Before It Breaks
T-Mat Global's capacity management framework for managed DevOps clients implements the proactive scaling approach that prevents capacity-related incidents — rather than the reactive scaling approach that addresses capacity constraints after they produce latency degradation or availability incidents. The framework covers: capacity trend analysis (monthly review of infrastructure resource utilization trends against the projected growth trajectory, with scaling recommendations before utilization reaches the threshold that produces performance degradation); peak load planning (pre-scaling for anticipated traffic events — product launches, marketing campaigns, seasonal peaks — based on historical traffic patterns and client-provided forward-looking business information); autoscaling validation (quarterly load testing to validate that autoscaling configurations respond correctly to the traffic ramp rates the client's traffic pattern produces); and cost efficiency review (quarterly review of resource allocation against actual utilization, with right-sizing recommendations that reduce infrastructure cost without compromising the availability SLA). Three Managed DevOps Failures That Cost Indian Enterprises in 2026

Managed Service Failure 1: SLA Without Engineering Accountability

The most common managed DevOps failure in India's IT outsourcing market: managed service agreements that define uptime SLAs without the engineering accountability model required to meet them. The managed service provider commits to 99.9% uptime. The infrastructure is not designed to the redundancy and failover standards required for 99.9% uptime. The uptime SLA is met on average by crediting downtime below the threshold rather than by engineering the infrastructure to eliminate single points of failure. When a production incident occurs, the root cause analysis reveals that the managed service provider had not implemented the redundancy controls required for the committed availability level. T-Mat Global designs infrastructure to the SLA standard before committing to the SLA — not the reverse.

Managed Service Failure 2: Managed Staffing Without Managed Operations

The staffing-based managed service model: the managed service provider assigns engineers to the client's infrastructure, the engineers operate the infrastructure using the client's runbooks and tooling, and the "managed" service is distinguished from direct staffing only by the billing model. When the engineers rotate (which they do, because the billing model incentivizes the managed service provider to rotate engineers to new engagements as their market rate increases), the operational knowledge rotates with them. The managed service that was supposed to free the client from knowledge retention risk replicates the same risk in the managed service provider's delivery model. T-Mat Global's managed service is a capability — not staffing — where the operational knowledge is in the infrastructure code and runbooks, not in the individual engineers who operate them.

Managed Service Failure 3: Reactive Managed Services With No Continuous Improvement

Managed DevOps services that are purely reactive — responding to incidents after they occur, applying patches when required, performing changes when requested — consistently deliver infrastructure that degrades over time relative to the production standard the client requires. The reactive managed service provider keeps the lights on. The proactive managed service partner improves the reliability, performance, and efficiency of the infrastructure continuously — implementing the post-incident improvements that prevent the same incident from recurring, the capacity optimizations that reduce infrastructure cost while maintaining availability, and the tooling upgrades that keep the delivery pipeline current with the evolving engineering standard. T-Mat Global operates the proactive model — because the T-Mobile USA production operations standard is not a snapshot that was current when T-Mat Global was founded but a continuously evolving benchmark that T-Mat Global applies to every managed service engagement.
